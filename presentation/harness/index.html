Flower's agent harness provides the runtime around an AI agent: executing its application, connecting it to a model, and carrying its responses and run state. We built Fusion Investigator on that foundation. It is a research assistant for experimental physicists, diagnostic specialists, and facility teams deciding what to measure next between experiments. Imagine a tokamak responds weakly to heating. Is heat escaping faster, or did less power reach the plasma? Our application lets an investigator consult partner evidence through controlled tools, while each site's gateway decides what can be released.

The investigation starts with a research question and configured site tools. In this demo, local analysis consumes device parameters and synthetic temperature, density, and heating profiles. The model receives approved summaries: machine context, a transport band, eleven rounded temperature samples, and power-audit findings. The gateway keeps the underlying arrays out of those responses. The intended output is a report that cites evidence, explains uncertainty, and recommends the next measurement. Alongside it, the application records tool results, including releases, refusals, and evidence identifiers, so researchers can inspect the basis for the recommendation.

This is a strong fit when facilities want to share useful findings while retaining control over disclosure. Central analysis requires permission to pool the data. A prompt alone cannot enforce access rules. A fixed script is useful for repeatable questions; an agent can choose its next request from the evidence it receives. Our design combines that flexibility with a deterministic gateway: prerequisites, five disclosure units per site, cached releases, and applicability checks. A steward model's approval cannot override these rules. That is the architectural advantage. Whether agent-directed investigation improves research outcomes still needs comparison with the fixed workflow.
